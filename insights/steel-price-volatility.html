Steel Price Volatility: Options for Procurement Teams

Steel prices have swung more than 60 percent peak-to-trough in three of the last five years. Procurement teams that manage steel as a spot buy absorb that volatility entirely. Those that do not manage to eliminate it, but can reduce it materially.

Varadha Narayanan July 2026 8 min read

Steel is not a commodity that procurement teams can treat as stable input cost. Between 2020 and 2025, hot-rolled coil prices in Asia moved from under $500 per tonne to over $900, back to $600, up again past $800, and then down toward $500 within the span of a single contract cycle. A construction or manufacturing business whose steel budget was set at the beginning of the year and whose contracts did not include price adjustment provisions absorbed losses that — depending on steel intensity — could run into tens of millions of dollars in a bad year.

The source of this volatility is not a single factor but a cascade of interacting ones, each operating on a different cycle length. Understanding the structure of steel price volatility is a precondition for choosing the right mitigation approach — because different instruments hedge different sources of volatility, and deploying the wrong instrument wastes both cost and management attention.

What drives steel price volatility

Iron ore and coking coal — the two primary raw material inputs to blast furnace steelmaking — account for roughly 40–50 percent of integrated steelmaker production costs. Both are themselves globally traded commodities with volatile spot markets. Iron ore prices have ranged from $80 to $230 per tonne in the last five years; coking coal from $110 to $670 per tonne following Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the Australian supply disruption. Steel prices lag these input movements by six to twelve weeks, the time required for raw material to move through the production process and reach the market.

Energy costs — primarily natural gas and electricity for electric arc furnace producers, and coking coal for blast furnace producers — add a second volatile input layer. The 2021–2022 European energy price shock raised production costs for European steelmakers by $150–200 per tonne and drove capacity idling that tightened global supply even as demand softened.

China's role is structural rather than cyclical. China produces approximately 50–55 percent of global steel output. When Chinese domestic demand contracts — as it did sharply in 2022–2023 with the property sector slowdown — the surplus is redirected to export markets at prices that reflect Chinese producers' need to run capacity rather than a market-clearing price. Chinese export surges have repeatedly reset global steel prices downward in ways that defy short-term supply-demand analysis.

Trade measures — anti-dumping duties, safeguard tariffs, quota systems — create regional price differentials and determine how quickly Chinese surplus reaches specific markets. EU steel prices are substantially insulated from Chinese competition by the safeguard quota system; Indian prices are partially insulated by import duties; GCC markets are more exposed. Understanding the specific trade policy environment for the markets you source from and sell into determines which pricing signals are relevant to your procurement position.

Procurement options and their trade-offs

Fixed-price annual contracts are the default procurement approach for steel buyers. The appeal is administrative simplicity: one price per specification per year, budgeting is straightforward. The structural problem is that fixed-price steel contracts transfer price risk entirely to the supplier. When steel prices rise after contract signing, suppliers face margin compression and may respond through quality degradation, delivery delays, or attempts to renegotiate. When prices fall, the buyer is locked into above-market rates and faces erosion of competitive cost position.

Fixed-price contracts work reasonably well in low-volatility environments. In the current environment, where annual price swings of 20–40 percent are common, they introduce a contract-versus-market dynamic that creates relationship stress precisely when you need supplier cooperation most.

Index-linked pricing is the structurally superior alternative. Under index-linked contracts, the base steel price moves with a defined external reference — the Steel Index, CRU Midwest HRC index, or a regional equivalent — within a pre-agreed price band. The buyer receives market pricing; the supplier retains margin relative to their costs rather than their contract. Both parties share market risk rather than one party bearing it entirely.

The practical challenge with index-linked contracts is selecting the right index. The CRU Hot-Rolled Coil North European index is appropriate for European-origin steel; it is irrelevant if you are buying from Indian or Chinese mills where pricing reflects a different cost and trade structure. Procurement teams need to understand which indexes their suppliers actually use for their own cost benchmarking before specifying a reference price.

"Index-linked contracts do not reduce the cost of steel. They reduce the cost of being wrong about steel prices — which is a different and more valuable thing for a manufacturer whose core competency is not commodity trading."

Hedging via futures is available for hot-rolled coil through the London Metal Exchange and the CME Group. For buyers with sufficient volume and treasury sophistication, steel futures allow locking in a forward price that eliminates short-term market risk. The cost is the futures premium over spot — which reflects market expectations of future price direction — plus hedging administration and the need for a treasury function capable of managing a commodity derivatives position.

In practice, steel futures hedging is used by large industrial companies and trading houses, not by most manufacturing or construction buyers in the GCC and India. Volume thresholds matter: the LME steel contracts are sized at twenty tonnes per lot, which is manageable, but the administrative and accounting requirements for a derivatives hedging programme are meaningful.

Supplier partnerships — long-term relationships with integrated steelmakers that provide volume commitment in exchange for supply security and pricing transparency — are the most effective mitigation for buyers with sufficient scale. The offer to a supplier is predictability of demand, which has real value to a capital-intensive producer managing rolling schedule optimisation. The return to the buyer is access to the supplier's cost structure for negotiation purposes and priority allocation during tight markets.

Demand aggregation — combining steel procurement volumes across business units or through a buying consortium — increases negotiation leverage and access to index-linked or partnership pricing tiers that are only available above certain volume thresholds. For mid-sized manufacturers in the GCC that lack the scale for direct mill relationships, industry associations and commodity procurement consortia offer a route to pricing structures that are otherwise inaccessible.

Timing market entry

The question procurement teams frequently ask is whether to time steel purchases to catch price troughs. The honest answer is that consistent price timing is not achievable for most organisations: the information advantage required is not available to non-specialist buyers, and the organisational disciplines required — holding off purchases when operational teams need material, buying forward when the business does not want to carry inventory — conflict with normal operating practice.

What is achievable is a rules-based approach that takes advantage of identifiable pricing conditions without requiring market prediction. A procurement policy that permits forward purchasing of an additional 30–60 days of cover when prices are below a defined moving average, and restricts forward purchasing when prices are above it, does not require forecasting skill — only discipline in execution. This smooths purchase price over a cycle without requiring the organisation to bet on price direction.
