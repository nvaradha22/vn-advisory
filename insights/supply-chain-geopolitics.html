Supply Chain Restructuring Under Geopolitical Pressure

Geopolitical pressure on global supply chains is not a temporary condition. It is the operating environment. Companies that treat restructuring as a project will repeat it. Companies that treat it as a capability will not.

Varadha NarayananJune 20267 min readSupply Chain

The phrase "supply chain restructuring" has been in circulation since 2020. COVID exposed single-source dependencies. The Russia–Ukraine war disrupted grain, fertiliser, and energy flows. Red Sea attacks added 14 days to Asia–Europe transits. US–China tariff escalations reshaped electronics and semiconductor procurement. Each event produced a wave of strategy documents and executive commitments to diversification.

Most of those commitments remained documents. The reason is consistent: restructuring a supply chain is expensive, slow, and operationally disruptive. The business case requires a sufficiently credible threat to justify the cost. And so companies have waited — through each disruption — for conditions to normalise before committing capital to structural change.

That calculation has now broken down. Geopolitical instability is no longer episodic. It is a permanent feature of the operating environment for any company with cross-border supply chains.

What pressure actually requires

Supply chain restructuring under geopolitical pressure is not the same as ordinary supplier diversification. Ordinary diversification — splitting volumes across multiple suppliers in the same country, or the same region — does not address country-level risk. A company with three Indian suppliers is still exposed to an India-specific shock. A company with five Chinese electronics suppliers is not diversified against US–China trade escalation.

Genuine restructuring requires three things that conventional supplier management does not:

  • Country-level risk mapping. Understanding which countries sit in your supply base, and what the realistic failure modes are for each — sanctions, export controls, port closure, currency controls, conflict spillover.
  • Tier-2 and tier-3 visibility. Most companies know their direct suppliers. Far fewer know where those suppliers source critical inputs. A tier-1 supplier in Vietnam may source 80 percent of its raw material from China. The country diversification is nominal.
  • Operational flexibility, not just geographic flexibility. Having approved alternative suppliers is not enough if lead times, minimum order quantities, and quality certification requirements make switching operationally impossible within a crisis timeline.

"A company with three Indian suppliers is still exposed to an India-specific shock. Geographic diversification that stops at tier-1 is mostly accounting."

The GCC–India corridor: specific considerations

For companies operating supply chains across the GCC and India, the relevant geopolitical pressures are particular. They are not the same pressures affecting Taiwan Strait routing or US–China technology supply chains, though those can have indirect effects.

The corridor-specific risks that currently warrant active management include:

Strait of Hormuz concentration. Approximately 20 percent of global oil and LNG transits the Strait of Hormuz. For GCC-based manufacturers whose energy cost is tied to domestic gas prices, a Hormuz disruption scenario affects not just shipping but production cost. Companies should model the production-side impact, not only the logistics-side impact.

India's export control posture. India has demonstrated willingness to impose sudden export restrictions on agricultural commodities and, in some cases, manufactured goods. For buyers of Indian-origin rice, wheat, sugar, onions, and pharmaceutical ingredients, this represents a recurring sourcing risk that geographical diversification must account for.

US secondary sanctions exposure. GCC entities with Iran-adjacent trade relationships face heightened secondary sanctions risk under current US enforcement posture. This affects banking relationships, insurance access, and vessel availability — not just the directly sanctioned trade.

What a genuine restructuring programme looks like

Restructuring that addresses these risks — rather than producing a map that gets filed and forgotten — requires a programme design with three phases.

Phase 1: Exposure quantification

Before deciding what to change, companies need an accurate picture of what they have. This means a structured review of the supply base by country of production (not country of supplier registration), identification of single-source dependencies at tier-1 and tier-2, and a geopolitical risk scoring of each country in the footprint. The output is a risk-ranked exposure map, not a generic country risk matrix.

Phase 2: Prioritised restructuring decisions

Not all exposures warrant restructuring. The cost of diversification must be weighed against the probability and impact of the risk event. High-value, hard-to-substitute inputs from politically volatile countries warrant immediate action. Low-value, fungible commodities with multiple global sources warrant monitoring, not investment.

Phase 3: Operational validation

Approved alternative suppliers who have never received a purchase order are not real alternatives. They are a list. Operational validation means placing trial orders, completing quality qualification cycles, and testing the switching logistics under non-crisis conditions. Companies that have done this can switch in weeks. Companies that have not will find that the approval process alone takes longer than most crisis windows.

The work is operational, not strategic. It requires procurement and operations functions to own it — not strategy teams to document it. That is the consistent difference between companies that restructured successfully and those that did not.
