China+1 Sourcing: Operational Requirements

China+1 is a procurement strategy with sound logic and difficult execution. The countries that appear on China+1 lists — Vietnam, India, Bangladesh, Indonesia — each have specific operational constraints that determine whether diversification is real or nominal.

Varadha Narayanan July 2026 9 min read
Share Share on LinkedIn

China+1 entered procurement vocabulary as a geopolitical risk management strategy and has remained there through successive justifications: first the US-China trade war and tariff escalation from 2018, then COVID-19 supply concentration risk from 2020, then technology export controls and decoupling narratives from 2022 onward. The underlying logic — that over-concentration of supply in a single country creates systemic vulnerability — is correct. The execution challenge is that China's position in global manufacturing supply chains is not the result of geography or government preference. It is the result of decades of infrastructure investment, supplier ecosystem development, and workforce skill accumulation that alternative locations have not replicated.

The practical question for procurement teams is not whether to diversify away from China but which categories can be credibly sourced from alternative origins, at what cost premium, with what lead time penalty, and with what supplier quality risk. These questions require country-specific and category-specific answers, not a company-wide mandate to reduce China exposure.

Why China+1 is harder than it looks

The efficiency of Chinese manufacturing supply chains rests on three things that are rarely available together in alternative locations: supplier ecosystem depth, infrastructure quality, and workforce scale.

Supplier ecosystem depth means that the sub-components, materials, and specialist services required for complex manufacturing are available nearby, often within the same industrial park or within a short logistics radius. A consumer electronics manufacturer in Shenzhen sources PCBs, injection-moulded plastics, lithium cells, connectors, and packaging from suppliers clustered within an hour's drive. Replicating this in Vietnam, India, or Indonesia requires either importing those inputs — which rebuilds the China dependency at a different tier of the supply chain — or waiting for the local supplier ecosystem to develop, which takes a decade or more.

Infrastructure quality — ports, roads, power supply, logistics services — determines the conversion of manufacturing capability into reliable, on-time delivery. A factory that produces goods efficiently but cannot get them to port reliably, or whose electricity supply is interrupted frequently enough to affect yield rates, does not offer a credible alternative to China regardless of its unit labour cost.

The minimum order quantity (MOQ) problem is frequently underestimated. Chinese factories at scale can offer low MOQs because their fixed costs are spread over enormous volumes. A factory producing 10 million units per year can offer trial runs of 5,000 units. A new factory in an alternative country building toward scale will typically require much higher MOQs to cover its fixed costs, which makes market entry more capital intensive and reduces the buyer's flexibility during ramp-up.

Vietnam: electronics, footwear, and rising costs

Vietnam has been the most successful China+1 destination, attracting significant FDI in electronics assembly (Samsung, Intel, LG), footwear, and garments. It benefits from proximity to China's supplier ecosystem, a relatively developed port infrastructure at Ho Chi Minh City and Hai Phong, and a young workforce. Vietnam's electronics exports grew from approximately $25 billion in 2015 to over $100 billion by 2023.

The constraints are real. Labour costs in Vietnam's industrial provinces are rising steadily — Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City are approaching wage levels comparable to lower-tier Chinese cities, eroding the cost advantage that initially attracted investment. Power supply reliability is improving but remains a risk for high-precision manufacturing. The supplier ecosystem for electronics remains shallow: most Vietnamese electronics assembly operations source components from China, Japan, and South Korea, which means Vietnam's role is final assembly rather than true manufacturing independence.

Vietnam's tariff agreements — CPTPP membership and the EU-Vietnam FTA — provide market access advantages for buyers routing production through Vietnam for sale in CPTPP or EU markets. But rules of origin requirements for these agreements typically require substantial local value addition, which loops back to the ecosystem depth problem.

India: pharmaceuticals, textiles, and the infrastructure gap

India's China+1 credentials are strongest in two categories: pharmaceuticals (where India is already the world's largest supplier of generic drugs by volume) and textiles (where cotton spinning and weaving capacity is substantial). In both categories, India has genuine depth — not just assembly but upstream supply chain capability.

Outside these categories, India's manufacturing competitiveness is constrained by infrastructure gaps and regulatory friction that have persisted despite sustained reform effort. Logistics costs in India as a share of GDP remain higher than China, reflecting inefficiencies in inter-state transport, port handling, and customs clearance. Power supply reliability varies significantly by state — Gujarat and Tamil Nadu are meaningfully better than the national average, while other manufacturing states are not. Land acquisition for greenfield industrial sites remains time-consuming.

"India has the workforce, the demographics, and increasingly the infrastructure investment. What it has not yet assembled at scale is the supplier ecosystem density that converts investment into reliable, competitive, high-volume manufacturing output. That will come — but on a decade timescale, not a procurement cycle."

The Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme, which provides financial incentives to manufacturers who achieve defined output and incremental sales thresholds in priority sectors, has attracted real investment in mobile phones, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, and specialty chemicals. Apple's supply chain shift toward India — Foxconn and Tata assembling iPhones in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu — is the most visible example. But these investments are in early scale-up; quality consistency and yield rates at the volumes required for a full Apple production transfer from China will take several more years to demonstrate.

Bangladesh: garments and concentration risk

Bangladesh is a mono-category China+1 story. It is the world's second-largest garment exporter after China, with a cost structure in basic apparel that no other country can currently match at scale. The Rana Plaza disaster of 2013 exposed serious compliance deficits that have since been substantially — though not universally — addressed through the Accord on Fire and Building Safety and the Alliance for Bangladesh Worker Safety.

The risk profile for buyers sourcing from Bangladesh is concentration risk of a different kind: over-exposure to a single country rather than to China. Bangladesh's garment sector is vulnerable to climate risk (flooding and cyclone exposure), political instability, and labour relations events. The 2024 political upheaval that displaced the Hasina government demonstrated that Bangladesh's political environment can shift abruptly in ways that disrupt normal business operations. Buyers who shifted garment production from China to Bangladesh to reduce concentration risk have partially replaced one concentration risk with another.

Indonesia: commodities and emerging manufacturing

Indonesia's China+1 story is primarily in commodities — nickel processing, palm oil, pulp and paper — rather than manufactured goods. Indonesia has used its position as the world's largest nickel producer to mandate domestic processing (the 2020 nickel ore export ban), attracting Chinese and Korean investment in nickel smelting and battery materials processing. This is a supply chain restructuring story driven by resource nationalism rather than cost arbitrage.

In manufacturing beyond commodities, Indonesia is an earlier-stage story than Vietnam or India. Labour costs are competitive, the domestic consumer market is large (280 million population), and government policy is directionally supportive of manufacturing. But logistics infrastructure outside Java remains underdeveloped, and the domestic supplier ecosystem for complex manufacturing is shallow.

The GCC angle: nearshore for Europe

For European buyers, the GCC — specifically the UAE's Jebel Ali free zone ecosystem and Saudi Arabia's developing industrial zones — offers a different kind of China+1 option: nearshore rather than labour-cost arbitrage. GCC manufacturing costs are not competitive with China or Southeast Asia on labour. But for products where proximity, flexibility, and supply chain responsiveness matter more than unit labour cost — custom fabrication, project materials for large infrastructure projects, pharmaceutical packaging, food manufacturing — the GCC can offer a credible alternative with shorter lead times and no Red Sea/Suez exposure.

What actually works

The China+1 strategies that produce real diversification share a common structure: they start with specific categories rather than company-wide mandates, they invest in supplier development in the alternative location before requiring volume, and they maintain China production at a defined share rather than attempting complete exit.

A 70/30 China/alternative split in a category, with the alternative supplier receiving consistent volume to build capability and reduce costs, is structurally more resilient than a 100/0 China position — and more commercially realistic than a mandated 50/50 split that forces a buyer to source from an alternative that is not yet competitive. The goal is an operational alternative, not a political statement.
