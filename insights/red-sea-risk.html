Red Sea, Hormuz, Malacca: Risk Profiles and Mitigation Options

The three chokepoints that concentrate the most global trade volume are not interchangeable risks. Each has a different probability profile, different mitigation options, and different cost implications for rerouting.

Varadha Narayanan July 2026 9 min read
Share Share on LinkedIn

Global maritime trade is organised around geography in ways that concentrate risk. Three waterways — the Bab-el-Mandeb strait at the southern end of the Red Sea, the Strait of Hormuz between the Gulf and the Gulf of Oman, and the Strait of Malacca between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra — collectively handle a share of world trade that no realistic diversification strategy can route around entirely. Understanding the specific risk profile of each chokepoint is the necessary precursor to building a mitigation strategy that is proportionate rather than generic.

The important distinction is between disruption probability and disruption consequence. The Strait of Malacca has a higher baseline disruption probability than the Strait of Hormuz — but a full Hormuz closure would produce consequences of a different order of magnitude. Risk management that conflates the two will either over-invest in hedging low-consequence risks or under-invest in hedging low-probability, catastrophic ones.

Bab-el-Mandeb and the Red Sea corridor

The Bab-el-Mandeb strait, connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden, is approximately 29 kilometres wide at its narrowest point. It is the gateway to the Suez Canal route, which carries roughly 12–15 percent of global trade by value and a higher proportion of container traffic on the Europe-Asia corridor. Since late 2023, Houthi attacks on commercial vessels in the Red Sea have materially disrupted this route.

The Houthi campaign demonstrated that a non-state actor with anti-ship missiles, drones, and access to commercial AIS data can deter a significant proportion of container traffic from using a major trade route. By early 2024, most major container lines had suspended Red Sea transits and were routing via the Cape of Good Hope. The diversion adds approximately 10–14 days to Europe-Asia voyages and 7,000–9,000 additional nautical miles per round trip.

The cost implications are significant but not catastrophic for most cargo categories. Bunker fuel costs for the additional distance add approximately $300,000–500,000 per round trip for a large containership. The demand for vessel capacity to cover the same routes increases, which tightens the market and raises freight rates. Spot rates on Asia-Europe routes tripled from Q4 2023 to Q2 2024 before stabilising.

War risk insurance surcharges for Red Sea transits introduced an additional cost layer. Insurers priced Red Sea passage at 0.5–1.5 percent of cargo value for vessels that chose to transit rather than divert — a significant premium on high-value cargo. The combination of freight rate inflation, extended transit times, and insurance costs means that for most importers sourcing from Asia for European markets, the Red Sea disruption added $50–200 per twenty-foot equivalent unit to landed costs depending on product category and cargo value.

Strait of Hormuz: low probability, extreme consequence

The Strait of Hormuz is 33 kilometres wide at its navigable channel. Approximately 20 percent of global oil trade and 20 percent of LNG trade passes through it. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, Iraq, and Iran all export hydrocarbons through this corridor. The UAE and Saudi Arabia have invested in pipeline capacity that bypasses Hormuz — the UAE's Habshan-Fujairah pipeline can move approximately 1.5 million barrels per day to the Indian Ocean directly — but total bypass capacity remains a fraction of Hormuz throughput.

A full Hormuz closure is a low-probability event. Iran has periodically threatened closure but has not attempted it, because it would simultaneously affect Iran's own export revenues. The more realistic disruption scenarios involve partial interference — harassing tanker traffic, seizing vessels, mining the approaches — rather than physical blockade.

"The Strait of Hormuz is best understood as a tail risk with systemic consequences. A sustained closure would produce an oil price shock that no hedging programme designed for normal market conditions can fully absorb. The mitigation question is not how to hedge away the risk — it is how long you can sustain operations at two to three times current energy costs."

For GCC-based industrial buyers and exporters, Hormuz risk is not primarily a logistics problem — it is an energy supply problem. Manufacturing operations that depend on natural gas for energy or feedstock face input cost and availability risks that transit insurance cannot address. The practical mitigation involves fuel switching capability (where feasible), strategic gas reserves, and contractual provisions that trigger force majeure or price renegotiation if Hormuz is disrupted for more than a defined period.

Strait of Malacca: chronic risk, manageable mitigation

The Strait of Malacca connects the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea and handles approximately 30 percent of global seaborne trade — including roughly 80 percent of China's oil imports. At its narrowest point (the Phillips Channel), it is 2.7 kilometres wide. The alternative route, around Indonesia through the Sunda or Lombok Straits, adds two to four days to voyage times and is viable for most cargo types.

The risk profile of the Strait of Malacca is different from Hormuz. The primary historical risk has been piracy — the Strait was one of the world's most active piracy zones in the early 2000s, with incident rates that fell sharply after coordinated naval patrols by Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore, and Thailand were introduced in 2004. Current piracy risk is low compared to historical levels but not eliminated.

The more significant medium-term risk for Malacca is geopolitical: any serious military confrontation in the South China Sea involving China, Taiwan, the United States, or regional actors could disrupt or deny Malacca transits. This scenario is lower probability than Red Sea disruption but higher consequence for the global economy given the volume of trade involved — and is structurally more difficult to mitigate because the alternative routing options are less developed.

Practical mitigation options and their cost

For procurement and supply chain teams, the practical mitigation toolkit for chokepoint risk includes four main instruments: strategic safety stock, dual or contingency routing arrangements, war risk insurance, and contractual provisions.

Safety stock is the most universally applicable instrument. Adding two to four weeks of additional inventory for categories that depend on at-risk routes provides a buffer against transit time increases without requiring route changes. The carrying cost of additional safety stock at current financing rates is typically $15–40 per tonne per month depending on product density and storage costs — material but quantifiable. For most categories, this is cheaper than the disruption cost of a stockout.

Dual routing involves qualifying alternative sourcing or routing options before a disruption occurs. For Asian-origin cargo destined for the GCC or Europe, this means establishing relationships with suppliers in countries that can route via land corridors (India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor) or Pacific routings, or holding relationships with carriers that serve both Red Sea and Cape routes. The cost of maintaining these relationships is primarily administrative; the cost of activating them during a disruption is the premium freight rate minus the baseline.

War risk insurance covers physical loss or damage to cargo from war, terrorism, and related perils. Standard marine cargo insurance excludes these perils; war risk cover is purchased separately. Premium rates vary substantially with geopolitical conditions — Red Sea surcharges were essentially zero before 2023 and spiked to 0.5–1.5 percent of insured value during the Houthi campaign. Buyers of war risk insurance need to understand that rates are retrospective to current conditions, not forward-looking: premiums spike exactly when the risk is highest.

Contractual provisions — specifically, force majeure clauses that specify chokepoint disruption as a qualifying event, and price adjustment mechanisms that trigger when freight indices exceed defined thresholds — allow cost sharing between buyer and supplier during disruption events. These need to be negotiated before disruptions occur; attempting to invoke force majeure in an existing contract that does not specifically list the relevant trigger is likely to produce a dispute rather than a resolution.
